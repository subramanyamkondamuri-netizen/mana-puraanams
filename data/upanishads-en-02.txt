When humanity succumbed to materialism and sensory indulgence, Bhagavan Sri Krishna distilled the
essence of the Vedas as the Bhagavad Gita. Later, Sage Veda Vyasa codified the Vedas into four divisions:
Rigveda, Yajurveda, Samaveda, and Atharvaveda. Enlightened seers extracted their ultimate philosophy into
the Upanishads (Vedanta).

Later, Sri Adi Shankaracharya and spiritual masters like Swami Gnananda authored authoritative
commentaries on them. Studying the Upanishads expands intuitive perception, enriches understanding, and
fulfills life's purpose. While 108 Upanishads are preserved, 14 are regarded as foremost, with 10 primary texts
forming the core of philosophical study.

Each Veda comprises three operational divisions:

 1. Brahmanas: Technical expositions on the nature and attributes of the Divine.
 2. Aranyakas: Contemplative practices, inward worship, and ritual disciplines.
 3. Upanishads (Vedanta): The concluding philosophical essence synthesizing the former two.

The Upanishads clarify phenomena that elude standard scholarship. For example, in the process of sleep, our
physical limbs and senses rest first while the brain remains active with dreams; subsequently, the mind
recedes into dreamless sleep (Sushupti), before returning to waking consciousness (Jagruti).

They similarly distinguish three states of human gratification:

   Physical Comfort: Procured by shielding the body with clothing during winter or using climate control
   during summer.
   Mental Happiness: Experienced through worldly triumphs such as passing examinations, earning
   livelihood, or securing riches.
   Spiritual Bliss (Ananda): Awakened in the soul through selfless charity, relieving poverty, feeding the
   hungry, and communing with the Divine.                               2. MANDUKYOPANISHAD

The Mandukya is regarded by sages as the pinnacle of the Upanishads. It establishes the non-dual reality that
the Creator, creation, and the individual soul are fundamentally one, demonstrating this through the sacred
syllable OM.

The designation derives from Manduka (the frog). A frog remains concealed beneath dried mud layers for
nine to ten months of the year, emerging during the monsoons to call out. In like manner, ancient ascetics
immerse themselves in silent meditation for long durations, discover spiritual truth, and emerge into society
to instruct humanity. Furthermore, human consciousness alternates across waking, dreaming, and deep sleep
just as a frog leaps between states.

Belonging to the Atharvaveda, the Mandukya contains only 12 concise mantras, yet resolves the core problems
of ontology. Sri Gaudapadacharya composed his famous Mandukya Karika (215 verses) upon it, which was
further elucidated by Adi Shankaracharya.

PEACE INVOCATION (SANTHI MANTRA)


   "Om! O Gods, may we hear only auspicious things with our ears. O adorable Deities, may we see only
   noble things with our eyes. With firm limbs and sound bodies, may we offer praise and dedicate our
   lifespan to the service of the Divine. May Indra of ancient glory, the all-knowing Sun, Garuda the
   conqueror of evil, and Brihaspati the lord of wisdom bestow their blessings upon us! Om Peace! Peace!
   Peace!"


Cosmic aspects such as the dawn, oceanic tides, wind, and rain were revered as gods (Indra, Varuna, Vayu) by
our forefathers. Because Nature (Prakruti) sustains all living species with food and habitat, living in
equilibrium with nature is an essential obligation.

Individual manifestations are not separate from the Absolute. From one lump of clay, diverse utensils—plates,
cups, tumblers, and figurines—are fashioned. Individually they appear distinct, yet fundamentally they are
nothing but clay. Similarly, everything manifests from OM, rests in OM, and resolves back into OM.

THE FOUR DIMENSIONS OF CONSCIOUSNESS

 1. Waking Consciousness (Jagradavastha – Vaisvanarudu):
    Consciousness directed outward toward tangible phenomena. In this state, the self experiences external
    objects through 7 cosmic limbs (Heaven as the head, the Sun as the eyes, Air as the breath, Space as the
    torso, Water as the kidneys, Fire as the digestive hearth, and Earth as the feet) and 19 instruments of
    perception (5 sense organs, 5 motor organs, 5 vital breaths, along with mind, intellect, memory-store, and
    ego).
 2. Dream Consciousness (Swapnavastha – Taijasudu):
    The physical senses are dormant, but the subtle mind remains active. The conscious mind rests while the
    subconscious constructs an inner universe. Joys and sorrows are experienced internally through dreams
    arising from daily impressions, unfulfilled desires, or intuitive premonitions.
 3. Deep Dreamless Sleep (Sushupti – Prajnudu):
    Both outer sensory awareness and inner mental projections cease. The mind folds into unified   consciousness characterized by the pure "I-sense". If several individuals sleep in the same room and one is
   addressed by name, only that specific person awakens, proving the persistence of this subtle identity.
   Undifferentiated rest and comfort are experienced here, making it the threshold between conscious
   dimensions.
 4. The Transcendental Fourth (Turiya – The Soul):
   Unconditioned, uncaused, non-dual, changeless, and utterly peaceful. It is the silent, pure witness
   underlying the three transient states. Just as the Sun illuminates human affairs without engaging in their
   virtues or vices, the Soul observes physical, mental, and subconscious events without becoming
   contaminated by them.

THE COMPOSITION OF OM

   'A' corresponds to the expansive waking dimension (Vaisvanarudu).
   'U' corresponds to the intermediate subtle dream dimension (Taijasudu).
   'M' corresponds to the causal deep sleep dimension (Prajnudu), which absorbs the preceding states.
   The Silence (Amatra) following the chant represents the immutable fourth state—the unconditioned
   Atman.

By meditating upon OM as an unmoving thumb-sized flame within the lotus of the heart, the spiritual center
unfolds and reveals divine awareness.


                                        Declaration of the Rishi:
     "The world is God; all living beings are God; OM is God; and therefore, Me – The God!"                                  3. EESAVASYOPANISHAD

The Upanishads present the verified realizations of the ancient seers. The seeker in this scripture moves
beyond asking for guidance from darkness to light; he petitions to penetrate through that very light to behold
the ultimate truth resting beyond the celestial brilliance.

Comprising 18 verses, the text takes its name from its opening declaration: "Eesa vasyam idam sarvam"
(Everything in this universe is enveloped by God). It was delivered by Sage Atharva to his son.

PEACE INVOCATION (SANTHI MANTRA)


   "Om! That Supreme Reality is Whole; this manifested universe is Whole. From the Whole, the Whole
   emerges. When the Whole is drawn from the Whole, the Whole alone remains undiminished."


This principle is illustrated through three examples:

 1. Countless lamps can be ignited from a primary flame without reducing the original lamp's intensity or
    luminosity.
 2. A person can distribute love unconditionally among many individuals without depleting the reservoir of
    affection within the heart.
 3. A flowering tree brings forth numerous blossoms year after year, each complete in form and scent, without
    diminishing the generative vitality of the tree.

The Peace Invocation repeats the word Santhi thrice to nullify three categories of impediments:

    Adhyatmika: Physical illnesses and psychological agitations.
    Adhibhautika: Injury or friction caused by other creatures.
    Adhidaivika: Calamities triggered by natural forces like floods, lightning, and wildfires.

KEY PRINCIPLES

    Sanctified Living and Renunciation: Recognize that the entire universe belongs to the Supreme.
    Enjoy life through mental detachment, coveting no person's wealth. Behold the Divine within family,
    possessions, and worldly responsibilities.
    The Path of Detached Action (Karma): Aspire to live a full span of one hundred years performing
    duties selflessly. Dedicated actions performed without selfish desires leave no karmic residue. Those who
    disregard the inner spirit and cater exclusively to bodily indulgences are designated "slayers of the Self"
    (Atmahantah), caught in iterative cycles of sorrow.
    The Nature of the Soul: The Self is motionless yet faster than thought. The physical senses cannot
    overtake it because it is already present at every destination. It is both near and far, within everything and
    outside everything. One who discovers all beings within the Self and the Self within all beings rises above
    malice, delusion, and sorrow.
    Harmony of Action and Meditation: Secular competence (Avidya) overcomes physical want and
    mortality, while spiritual wisdom (Vidya) confers immortality. Both disciplines must operate in complete
    balance.
